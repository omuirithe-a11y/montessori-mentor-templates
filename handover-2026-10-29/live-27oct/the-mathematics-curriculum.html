Introduction

The Montessori Mathematics curriculum provides children with a deep understanding of mathematical concepts through a combination of tactile materials, exploration and discovery by creating opportunities for them to learn at their own pace, driven by their innate curiosity and motivation. The curriculum is divided into distinct groups and stages, each building on the previous one, allowing for a progressive understanding of mathematical ideas. The first stage focuses on numeracy, where children learn about numbers through the use of materials such as the Number Rods and Sandpaper Numerals, which provide tactile experiences that help children develop a solid foundation in number recognition. As children progress, they are introduced to more complex concepts such as the decimal system using materials like the Golden Beads, which enable them to physically manipulate quantities, fostering a concrete understanding of place value. This foundational understanding is crucial as children move on to operations such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division, the curriculum encouraging a deep understanding of mathematical concepts, rather than rote memorization.

A key feature of Montessori mathematics is the use of self-correcting materials, which allow children to learn from their mistakes without the direct intervention of a teacher, fostering problem-solving skills and independence. The curriculum is designed to be flexible, allowing each child to progress at their own pace, an individualized approach which ensures that each child's unique learning style and cognitive development are respected and supported.

In summary, the Montessori method makes math tangible. The materials are hands-on tools designed to help children understand abstract mathematical concepts such as quantity, place value and operations in a tangible way, by manipulating a range of specially designed materials. Children handle physical representations of numbers (79, 3000, ½) and by physically handling, combining, and sharing the quantities they construct an understanding of fundamental arithmetic concepts such as addition, subtraction and division. They learn through their senses, an idea which can be traced back to Greece, 2300 years ago.

The Peripatetic Axiom

In 335BC, Aristotle founded the Peripatetic School of philosophy in Athens and argued that universal meanings could be drawn from specific empirical data. Thomas Aquinas, an Italian monk priest and theologian to Pope Pope Clement IV, described as the most influential medieval thinker in the western tradition, drew on the Aristotelean tradition when in 1259 he wrote:

"Nihil est in intellectu quod non sit prius in sensu."

“There is nothing in the intellect that didn´t first pass through the senses.”

Maria Montessori placed herself firmly in this intellectual lineage:

"The senses, being explorers of the world, open the way to knowledge. Our apparatus for educating the senses offers the child a key to guide his explorations of the world, they cast light upon it which makes visible to him more things in greater detail than he could see in the dark, or uneducated state."

- Maria Montessori, The Absorbent Mind, p.167. (1949)

This is quite a different approach to traditional methods of educating children.

The Importance of Indirect Preparation

Montessori classes divide children according to mixed-age groups: 3 to 6, 6 to 9, and 9 to 12. In a primary classroom, the children are generally introduced to mathematics at the end of their first year or (more often) at the beginning of the second year, when they are aged 4. Before then, a great deal of preparation takes place, in the following areas:

  • Fine Motor Skills
  • Control and Coordination of Movement
  • Logic and Sequencing
  • Teamwork
  • Base 10
  • Measurement
  • Squares and Cubes
  • Geometry
  • Pre-Algebra

I once worked in a school where a pair of 3-year-old twins joined in August; one of the buys was placed in my class and his brother was placed in another class. At Christmas we had the first Parent-Teacher conference and the parents asked why the boy in my class hadn’t yet begun mathematics, while his brother had already been presented with the chains in Group 3. What to say? Because of professional courtesy I couldn’t tell them the truth, but the words did go through my head: “Because his brother’s teacher is a fool.”

Many teachers begin mathematics when the child isn’t ready and hasn’t had enough experience with the exercises of Practical Life and the Sensorial activities, because the teacher feels pressure from management or parents or both. This is a pity and one of many examples of what for me is the biggest failing in the Montessori world: a lack of guidance. Teachers don’t receive the right guidance at the right time, and as a consequence the children don’t receive the right guidance at the right time.

As chance would have it, the following summer the class with the other twin was mixed with my class for a month (because most families had gone on holiday, so the school preferred to pay half the teachers and assistants while the other half took an unpaid break) so I had an opportunity to see for myself what unprepared children look like. The results weren’t pretty, as seen in the photos to the right.

The children in my class all began Mathematics in their second year, but they made great progress in a very short amount of time because they were well-prepared during their first year. During that year I had faith in what they could achieve, and while I understand why many Montessori teachers have doubts, I also believe that if they had more faith in the Montessori approach (and knew how to implement it), they’d be much happier, as would the children they guide.

Group 1 - Numbers from 1-10

Group 1 lays the foundation for all later mathematical learning. Children discover that numbers are not just abstract figures but representations of real, countable objects, a multi-sensory approach whereby the materials facilitate exploration of quantity and symbol. When a child holds the Number Rods, for example, they can physically see and feel the difference between “three” and “seven,” while the Sandpaper Numerals introduce the written symbols in a tactile, memorable way.

Through repetition and exploration, children learn one-to-one correspondence, quantity recognition, numerical order, and the association between number symbols and their values. This multisensory learning process ensures that abstract concepts are firmly rooted in concrete understanding. By the end of Group 1, the child not only knows how to count from 1 to 10 but also understands what these numbers truly represent. This confidence and clarity provide a strong mathematical foundation, preparing the child for the next stages of Montessori math, where they will explore the decimal system and operations with larger numbers.

Number Rods

A concrete representation of quantities from 1 to 10.

Materials

10 wooden rods identical to the Red Rods in dimension; the shortest rod is red, and the successive rods are red and blue in alternate decimetres, the number of sections representing the number of rods.

Aims

Sensorial experience of the quantities of each number and their names.
Understanding that each number is represented by a single object, as a whole separate from the others.

Memorisation of the sequence of numbers from 1 to 10.

Presentation

Begins with rods placed at random on the mat.

  1. Invite child to lay out the rods in sequence.
  2. Begin with the rod of one, keeping red rods aligned on the left.
  3. When all rods are in order, have a three-period lesson, using the first three rods.

Exercise 1 - Ask for a rod

For example Bring me 2. After the child brings the rod, have her check by counting it.

Exercise 2 - Identify a rod

Choose any rod and ask the child, What is this?

Control of Error

The segments of the rod act as a guide.

Age

Around 4, after much sensorial work.

Notes

The remaining rods are introduced three at a time, in sequence and re-presenting any numbers that the child has forgotten. Finish presenting all 10 rods before progressing to Exercise 1.

Sandpaper Numerals

An abstract representation of quantities from 1 to 10.

Materials

Sandpaper Numerals from 0 to 9 on boards.

Sensitising Tray.

Direct Aim

Learning the symbols for the numbers already known.

Indirect Aim

Preparation for writing.

Presentation
  1. Wash hands, then sensitise fingers.
  2. Choose three numerals, contrasting in shape and sound.
  3. Present by means of a three-period lesson, frequently encouraging the child to trace as well as say the numerals.
Exercise

The child’s own activity with the numerals, as per the presentation.

Control of Error

None, but the sandpaper acts as a guide.

Age

Around 4, after the Number Rods, but zero is presented after Spindle Boxes.

Number Rods and Cards

Makes the link between quantity and symbol, between concrete and abstract.

Materials

10 wooden rods identical to the Red Rods in length; the shortest rod is red, and the successive rods are red and blue in alternate decimetres, the number of sections representing the number of rods.

Direct Aims

Memorisation of the sequence of symbols.

Relation of the symbols to the quantities.

Indirect Aim

Preparation for addition, subtraction and -remotely- multiplication and division.

Presentation
  1. Ask the child to bring the rods and show how to place at random on the mat.
  2. Remove a few cards and ask the child to identify them.
  3. Ask the child to remove and name the remaining cards, placing each at random on the mat.
  4. Remove and introduce 10, saying This is ten.
  5. Ask the child to find the rod of ten by counting off the segments on the rods
  6. Match by placing the card upon the last segment of the rod.
  7. Repeat, choosing random cards and asking the child to:
  • identify the symbol,
  • count the segments and
  • match the card with a rod.
  1. Continue until all rods and cards are matched.
  2. Remove cards from rods and place at random on the mat.
  3. Invite the child to again match the rods and cards.

Exercise 1 - Find a Rod (Bring Rods to Cards).

Set up two mats at a distance from each other with rods placed on one and cards on the other.

Have the child:

  • Isolate one card and identify the numeral,
  • Fetch the matching rod,
  • Check by counting the segments and match the card to the rod.

Exercise 2 - Find a Card (bring cards to rods).
Same steps as exercise 1 but bringing cards to rods.

Exercise 3 - Sequence.

  1. Ask the child to arrange the rods in order.
  2. Remove the cards at random and place upon the mat so they can all be seen.
  3. Isolate one card and find the matching rod by counting off the segments.
  4. Repeat, choosing cards at random and matching with rods.
  5. At the end, ask the child to read the numbers, first ascending from 1 to 10 and then descending from 10 to 1.
  6. Remove the cards and invite the child to again match cards and rods.
Note

At the end, encourage the child to read the numbers both ascending and descending.

Exercise 4 - Sensorial Impression of Addition, Multiplication and Division.

  1. Ask the child to place the rods on the lower part of the mat and place the matching card upon the last segment of each.
  2. Identify the rod and card of 10 and isolate by placing at the top of the mat.
  3. Identify and the rod and card of 9, placing beneath the 10-rod.
  4. Ask the child which number we need to add to the 9-rod in order to make 10.
  5. After he identifies it, place the 1 rod next to the 9-rod, demonstrating that they combine to make 10.
  6. Place the 8-rod beneath the 9 and ask which number you must add to it in order to make 10.
  7. After he identifies it, move the 2-rod next to the 8-rod, demonstrating that they combine to make 10.
  8. Continue in this way until only the 5-rod remains.
  9. Ask child what number we need to add to 5 in order to make 10.
  10. If he replies five, say Yes, five and five make ten and there are two fives in ten, flipping the rod over by pivoting it on its end as you say it.
  11. Remove the rod of 10.
  12. Invite the child to choose any number and explore it by placing at the top of the mat and then checking which combination of rods can form that number.

Exercise 5 - Subtraction
For example, after the child has made the 10’s (exercise 4) remove the rod of four and ask him to count what remains.

Control of Error

The sections on the rods act as a guide.

Age

About 4, immediately after the Sandpaper Numerals, when the child has developed familiarity with the symbols.

Tracing, Copying and Writing Numerals

Children require practice in writing the numerals, and we provide them with it in a variety of ways, some AMI-sanctioned, many not.

Spindle Boxes

The aims of The Spindle Boxes are:

Understanding that each symbol represents a separate quality of separate objects.

Understanding of zero and its symbol 0.

Reinforcement of the sequence of symbols.

Comprehension that there are no symbols apart from those between 0 and 9.

Numbers and Counters

The aims of Numbers and Counters are:

Reinforcement of the knowledge that each number is made up of separate quantities.

A verification of the sequence of numbers and how many separate units from each number.

Awareness of odd and even numbers.

Preparation for divisibility of numbers.

The Memory Game

Materials

11 pieces of paper folded up, each one with a number from 0 - 10.

55 small identical objects.

Presentation
  1. Introduce materials and form a group: up to a maximum of 11 children.
  2. Place the objects at a distance.
  3. Ask each child to choose a piece of paper and not to open it.
  4. When all have chosen, ask the first child to look at their paper, not revealing the number, then go get that quantity of objects.
  5. When they return, show them where to place the objects on the mat.
  6. Repeat the steps with the remaining children, keeping objects separate and numbers secret.
  7. At the end, the children take turns to individually reveal numbers and check by counting each other’s’ objects.

The Three-Period Lesson

The three-period lesson is a way of introducing new vocabulary.

In Group 1, we teach the names of the numbers using a three-period lesson, choosing three numbers differing in shape and sound.

First PeriodIntroductionThis is … 7 / 4 / 9

In the first period, we introduce the new name or information. During this period, the teacher must by definition play the most active role.

Second Period ExperienceShow me … 7 / 4 / 9

In the second period, the child gains experience with the names or materials, touching and moving them (put 7 here, touch 9, trace 4, give me 9 etc.). The teacher should aim to limit the vocabulary to the target language, by gesturing and only saying 7, 9 or 4, thus making it as clear as possible for the child by removing all superfluous language.

Third PeriodRecallWhat is this? “7.” And this? “9”. And this? “4”.

In the third period, the teacher checks whether the child has memorised the information.

Each of the groups of Mathematics functions as a three-period lesson, with the first presentations introducing the child to new information, the middle presentations giving the child opportunities to experience this new information, and the final presentation(s) checking whether the child has integrated this new information, as in this example using the activities in Group 1:

Group 2 - The Decimal System

Group 2 introduces children to the Decimal System, a foundational concept that helps them understand how numbers are organized and related by place value. Through concrete, hands-on materials, children explore the hierarchy of numbers -units, tens, hundreds, and thousands- building a clear and intuitive understanding of our base-ten system. They manipulate actual units, bars, squares, and cubes to represent 1’s, 10’s, 100’s, and 1000’s, forming a tangible bridge between the abstract and the concrete.

This activity develops both conceptual understanding and mathematical language. Children learn to name and combine quantities (e.g., one thousand, two hundred, thirty-four), practice exchanging (ten units for one ten bar), and eventually perform the four operations-addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division-within the decimal framework. By progressing from concrete exploration to abstract reasoning, the materials nurture precision, independence, and joy in mathematical discovery, laying the groundwork for a deep and lasting understanding of place value and operations, empowering children to move confidently into higher mathematical thinking.

General Group Aims

Consolidation of the meaning of the actions, i.e.

Addition means putting together.

Subtraction means removing a quantity so that a smaller quantity remains.

Multiplication means adding together the same number more than once.

Division means sharing equally.

Games and Extensions

The previous list doesn’t include any of the games and extensions which help the children to extend their interest in materials they have already mastered. Here are some examples:

Concrete to Abstract

Decimal Beads are a concrete representation of quantities from 1 to 1000, made up of:

  • Individual golden beads, each representing 1 unit,
  • Bars of 10, each bar made up of 10-unit beads,
  • Squares of 100, each square made up of 10 bars of 10, and
  • Cubes of 1000, each cube made up of 10 squares of 100.

Decimal Cards are an abstract representation of quantities from 1 to 1000, made up of a large set of cards with the symbols/numerals in the decimal colours:1-9 in Green 10-90 in Blue100-900 in Red 1000-9000 in Green

Decimal Beads and Cards are a combination of the two previous presentations, establishing the link between the concrete beads and the abstract numerals.

Decimal Operations

Once the child has assimilated the concrete and abstract quantities, they are introduced to the operations - addition, multiplication, subtraction and division. These are group exercises, with 2, 3 or 4 children collaborating to find the answer to an equation.

Dynamic Addition e.g. 2412 + 1253 + 3161 =

  1. The children each lay out the decimal cards (on individual mats) while the guide prepares a card with a math problem on it and gives it to the children.
  2. Child A goes to their mat and gets the cards representing 2412, then collects that quantity of beads on a tray.
  3. Child B goes to their mat and gets the cards representing 1253, then collects that quantity of beads on a tray.
  4. Child C goes to their mat and gets the cards representing 3161, then collects that quantity of beads on a tray.
  5. At the working mat, the children check the beads of the other children and -if they’re correct- places them on the mat. Once all of the numbers have been checked, the guide introduces the sign for addition [ + ] and says, “This is the sign for addition: addition means putting things together.”
  6. The children combine the units, count the sum, and get a Large Number Card to represent that sum ( 6 ).
  7. Next, they combine the tens:
  • demonstrate how to exchange ten bars of 10 for one square of 100,
  • count the remaining units
  • get a Large Number Card to represent that sum ( 2 ).
  1. The children combine the hundreds, count the sum, and get a Large Number Card to represent that sum ( 8 ).
  2. They combine the thousands, count the sum, and get a Large Number Card to represent that sum ( 6 ).
  3. The guide puts the cards together and verbalizes the process of addition:

“You brought two thousands, four hundreds, one ten and two units

You brought one thousand, two hundreds, five tens and three units

You brought three thousands, one hundred, six tens and one unit.

We put them together and now we have six thousands, eight hundreds, two tens and six units. Addition means ‘putting things together’.”

"This system in which a child is constantly moving objects with his hands and actively exercising his senses, also takes into account a child's special aptitude for mathematics. When they leave the material, the children very easily reach the point where they wish to write out the operation. They can thus carry out an abstract mental operation and acquire a kind of natural and spontaneous inclination for mental calculations."

- Maria Montessori, The Discovery of the Child, p. 279.

The photos below were taken in my class in Tokyo during one of the charitable fundraising events that the school held throughout the year. This group of children ranged from age 4 to 6 and had just counted all of the money (coins and notes) they’d raised. They used The Decimal Beads to count each of the categories (units, tens, hundreds and thousands) -converting them to the higher category as needed- then used the Large Number Cards to record the total, and then double-checked the total be counting everything again using The Stamp Game. Their grasp of mathematics was admirable, but more impressive was their ability to collaborate, each assuming a role in the team, working in harmony to achieve their goal.

Word Problems

Presentation
  1. Choose a word problem.
  2. Read it with the child.
  3. Set it up with the stamps, performing the operations as per the previous exercises.
  4. Continue as long as the child is interested.
Sample word problems:
  1. Nandini drives to Pallavakum four times every month. If she has been going for 11 months, how many times has Nandini driven to Pallavakum?
  2. Laura’s hens laid 54 eggs, and she wants to share them equally with her three friends. How many eggs will each friend receive?
  3. Peter had 6 balls and then he found 3 more. How many balls does he have now?
  4. Sean wants to read 7 books this summer. He has already read 4. How many more books does he have to read to meet his goal?
  5. Roger picked 7 apples from the tree. Each apple has 6 seeds. How many seeds are there in total?
  6. There were 49 leaves on the ground. If 7 children each picked up the same number of leaves, how many leaves did each child get?

Group 3 - Counting

Typically introduced around ages 4½ to 5, this group focuses on linear and skip counting, helping children move beyond simple enumeration to recognize numerical patterns and relationships. Key materials include the Teens and Tens Boards, which visually and tactilely demonstrate how numbers from 10 to 19, then up to 100, and then up to 1000 are constructed from combinations of units and tens. The Golden Beads and Number Cards are used together to reinforce the association between quantity and symbol, while the Hundred Board and Bead Chains (short and long) support linear and skip counting (e.g., counting by 2s, 5s, or 10s). These activities not only solidify number recognition but also lay the groundwork for multiplication and later work with the decimal system.

Linear Counting

Materials

A 100 square.

9 narrow green arrows with the numbers 1-9.

9 medium blue arrows with the numbers 10-90.

1 wide red arrow with the number 100.

A 1000-chain made of 100 bars of ten joined together.

Ten 100 squares.

A 1000 cube.

9 narrow green arrows with the numbers 1-9.

90 medium blue arrows with the numbers 10-90, 110-190, 210-290…910-990.

9 wide red arrows with the numbers 100-900.

1 extra wide green arrow with the number 1000.

Aims

Practice in counting.

Consolidation of the knowledge of counting.

Awareness of the striking difference between 100 and 1000.

Exercises

Presentation - 100 Chain.

Exercise - 1000 Chain.

Once the child has counted all of the short chains one by one, they are encouraged to count all of them at the same time.

"I have had a chain made by joining ten ten-bead bars end to end. This is called the "Hundred Chain". Then, by means of short and very flexible connecting links I had ten of these hundred chains put together, making the "thousand chain" These chains are of the same colour as the ten-beads. The difference in their reciprocal length is very striking. Let us first put down a single bead; then a ten-bead bar, which is about 7 cm. long; then a hundred-bead chain, which is about 70 cm. long; and finally, the thousand-bead chain which is about 7 meters long. The great length of this thousand-bead chain leads directly to another idea of quantity. The flexible connections between the different hundred lengths of the 1000 bead chain permit its being folded so that the "hundred chains" lie one against the other, forming in their entirety a long rectangle. The same quantity which formerly impressed the child by its length is now, in its broad, folded form, presented as a surface quantity."

- Maria Montessori, ‘The Advanced Montessori Method’, Vol. II’, pp. 189-190. (1916)

Two presentations that are not in my AMI album but which I have found to be very useful and much enjoyed by the children are the 100 Board and How to use a Measuring Tape.

100 Board

This is one of those activities that you see in every AMS classroom but never in AMI classrooms, which I really don’t understand: in Group 2 Decimal Beads transition into stamps, but in Group 3 the beads in the chains shouldn’t transition into the tiles of the 100 board? I prefer to follow the child, and the way the children universally enjoy this activity and create interesting variations upon it demonstrates it’s utility. Some examples:

How to use a Measuring Tape

When they begin measuring, the children will invariably start off small, measuring a tray or a lid of a box, and then proceed to measuring everything in the room, including tables, doors, windows, walls and even their classmates, if they sit still for a moment. So, this activity is a wonderful and fun way to demonstrate the practical utility of numbers.

In Group 5, the child works towards abstract addition, subtraction and multiplication, using materials that encompass a small and large abacus, seven wooden solids representing hierarchical values from one to one million, and a set of racks, tubes and dishes in the decimal colours, in tandem with the Unit Division Board.

Summary

The Montessori Mathematics Curriculum is a radically different approach to teaching mathematics, utilising tactile materials and a child-cantered learning philosophy to foster a deep understanding and appreciation of mathematical concepts. By creating opportunities for children to explore and discover mathematical ideas at their own pace, the curriculum not only supports the development of mathematical skills but also instills a lifelong love of learning, preparing them to be confident and competent learners, capable of tackling future complex mathematical challenges with enthusiasm and curiosity.

“I obtained most surprising results through their application, but I must confess that, while my efforts showed themselves in the intellectual progress of my pupils, a peculiar form of exhaustion prostrated me.

It was as if I gave to them some vital force from within me.”

- Maria Montessori.
