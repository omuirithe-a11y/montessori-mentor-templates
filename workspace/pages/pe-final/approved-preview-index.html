Introduction

Maria Montessori opened her first school in the San Lorenzo district of Rome, on 6 January 1907, and she called it a ‘Casa dei Bambini’, which translates from the Italian as either a ‘House of Children’ or ‘Home of Children’:

“We do not say that we want to prepare a school for the child; we wish to prepare an environment for life, where the child can develop a life. That is why we call this institution a House of Children. The idea is to give the children a house of their own, a house in which they are the masters of themselves.”

- Maria Montessori, Creative Development of the Child Vol. 1, pg. 52-53.

“We Italians have elevated our word ‘casa’ to the almost sacred significance of the English word ‘home,’ the enclosed temple of domestic affection, accessible only to dear ones.”

- Maria Montessori, The Discovery of the Child, pg. 37.

Despite its location in a slum neighbourhood (not far from Termini, the main train station in Rome), the casa was spotlessly clean, as befits a room prepared by someone with medical training. More than a century later, it remains a Montessori school, which I visited a few years ago, and took this photo. Here it was that Maria Montessori created an environment that could meet the physical, psychological, social, emotional, and cognitive needs of children at each stage of their development. She called it ‘the prepared environment’ and it is of paramount importance in the Montessori system of education:

The teacher only puts the child in direct contact with the environment, showing him how to use various things. […] In our schools the environment itself teaches the children.

- Maria Montessori, The Child in the Family, p. 64.

In the intervening century her approach has spread throughout the world -there are more than 15,000 Montessori schools, on six continents- but it can be adapted to any culture or setting, so all authentic Montessori environments evince the same key characteristics:

  • They are designed to support the developmental characteristics and interests of a mixed age group within a defined age range (0 to 3, 3 to 6, etc),
  • They facilitate self-directed, highly personalised learning and growth, and
  • They foster the child's self-motivation, natural curiosity and desire to learn, not merely permitting exploration and discovery but actively encouraging it.

In an environment such as this, the children engage with the activities according to their individual interests and passions, they feel at ease and thereby motivated to explore, discover and learn at their own pace, and exhibit a lively and purposeful engagement with the materials.

The prepared environment can become the guide’s most valuable assistant. The guide that wants to give her group a great deal of freedom yet does not want this freedom to degenerate into chaos, and one who wants to make her guidance more and more subtle, will find that time spent with the room and equipment [to create a prepared environment] pays ample dividends in improving her work.

- Peller, Lili. “The Children’s House.” On Education and Development of Young Children. New York: Philosophical Library, 1978. Repr. The NAMTA Journal 21.3 (1996, Summer): 8-23, pg. 21

Crucially, they find joy in learning, and as a result they exhibit confidence, self-discipline and concentration, develop a sense of responsibility and self-discipline, assume stewardship of the space, and demonstrate bonds of mutual respect and social connection. The first and most important responsibility of a Montessori guide, then, is to prepare the environment in a way that maximally facilitates learning:

“The teacher’s first duty is therefore to watch over the environment and this takes precedence over all the rest.”

- Maria Montessori, The Absorbent Mind, Chapter 27: ‘The Teacher's Preparation’, p. 277.

Preparing environments

I was fortunate in my first job at the Montessori School of Tokyo because the school was well established, the children in my class were mostly normalised, the parents tremendously supportive, and the environment had been prepared over the course of many years by experienced Montessori guides. As a result, I was able to focus on learning all of the things I needed to learn and not have to worry about the environment.

After I left Japan, I helped to prepare new environments in Russia and Thailand, and this was the first time I had to really think about this aspect of the job, my carpentry skills coming in handy in Moscow, where I built and installed the shelves. The following years were spent in China, where I worked in an established school in Beijing and set up a new class in Shanghai. In Switzerland I reorganised a classroom in Neuchatel, where the teacher had placed a line of shelves across the room, with a narrow passageway between them. When I asked why, she looked at me like I was simple, the answer was so obvious: “To stop the children from running.” Shortly afterwards she took sick leave -possibly worn out from chasing the children around the room- and didn’t return for four months (French), so I took the opportunity to remove the shelves and open the room up. Miraculously, the children didn’t run in the class.

After that, I established a new class in Geneva (photos here) and then another in Taiwan -which I write about below- and what follows are some of the lessons I’ve learned about preparing an environment, written in the order they need to be addressed.

Furniture - Purchasing

“The principal modification in the matter of school furnishings is the abolition of desks, and benches or stationary chairs. I have had tables made with wide, solid, octagonal legs, spreading in such a way that the tables are at the same time solidly firm and very light, so light, indeed, that two four-year-old children can easily carry them about. I also designed and had manufactured little chairs…We permit the child to select the position which he finds most comfortable as well as seat himself in his own place. And this freedom is not only an external sign of liberty, but a means of education. [Through such furnishings, the] child has learned to command his movements.”

- Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, p. 81-84. (1912)

I’ve spent more than half of my career in Asia, where it’s wonderfully simple to find someone who will create furniture to your exact specifications. Which is pretty great, obviously, but if you’re not so fortunate, in the world of Montessori, Nienhuis are the most famous brand, but they don’t come cheap, and I’ve not always been satisfied with either the durability or precision of their materials. Also, they are the first company I’ve seen provide a quotation for a table with a separate quotation for the legs, which I confess caused this ex-carpenter to raise an eyebrow. Some alternatives:

Gonzaga Arredi are based in northern Italy, not far from Parma and Reggio Emilia, and have a lot of experience in making furniture for many types of school, not just Montessori. Their catalogues are detailed and they even have a museum containing some of the original materials which were designed and used by Maria Montessori.

Northern California, as well as having some of the best wine in the world, is also home to Montessori Services, who have a pretty great selection of furniture, mats, trays, etc.

Kave Home began in Spain in 1983, but ship worldwide now, and have a lot of well-designed Montessori furniture

As do Voila Montessori

In the British Isles, the aptly named Montessori Materials are Irish and have a range of tables, chairs, mat storage stands, etc

In England, community playthings aren’t uniquely Montessori, but they have an array of beautiful furniture and when I’m in need of design or layout inspiration, I like to browse their catalogue.

In Asia there are quite a few companies producing Montessori (or Montessori-adjacent) furniture and materials, and although the quality can vary, the prices are a fraction of what you’ll pay in the west:

‘Montessori Teaching Aids’ are certified by AMI China

In Japan, Matsumoto Kagaku Kogo have many beautiful Asian-accented materials.

Furniture Dimensions

Rows of the Shelves

Montessori teachers add and remove activities throughout the year, according to the needs of the children, the frequency with which the materials are used, the season, etc, so being able to adjust the number and height of the rows in each shelving unit is useful. In addition, rent is a significant expense for most schools, so being able to fit a lot of materials into a small space makes adjustable shelving a good investment.

“Light furniture that he can carry about; low dressers within reach of his arms; locks that he can easily manipulate; chests that run on castors; light doors that he can open and shut readily; clothes-pegs fixed on the walls at a height convenient for him; brushes his little hand can grasp; pieces of soap that can lie in the hollow of such a hand; basins so small that the child is strong enough to empty them; brooms with short, smooth, light handles; clothes he can easily put on and take off himself; these are surroundings which invite activity, and among which the child will gradually perfect his movements without fatigue, acquiring human grace and dexterity.”

- Maria Montessori, The Advanced Montessori Method: Volume 1, p. 151. (2002)

Shelf Height

This depends on several factors: the first is the location of the school as children in some countries (Russia, for example) are distinctly bigger than others (Japan, for example) so I try and have the Metal Insets accessible for everyone: taking a few pencils shouldn’t be a test of fortitude for the shorter children. Secondly, who will be using the materials on each shelf: the first Practical Life and Sensorial shelves will mostly be used by 3-year-olds, while the Mathematics shelves holding the Essential Combination and Passage to Abstraction materials will be used by children aged 4 ½ to 5, who are quite a bit taller so it’s important to factor in which material will be on each shelf.

The materials are commonly laid out in order of presentation, so because the Geometry Cabinet is one of the first activities a 3-year-old will be introduced to, it’s often placed on the top shelf, which has sound pedagogic reasoning behind it but the consequence is that the smaller children have great difficulty in opening the drawers and choosing or even seeing the shapes of the insets. I prefer to place the Cabinet on one of the lower shelves so the child can look down on the open tray of shapes.

Shelf Length

The longest Red Rod and Number Rod are one metre, so the shelf should be at least 1.1 metre long, and for reasons of aesthetics and consistency, it’s better if all the shelves are the same length. It’s also possible to use the stand that is designed to hold the Red Rods, which has its advantages, specifically: it is portable.

“But in our specially prepared environments we see them all at once fix themselves upon some task, and then their excited fantasies and their restless movements disappear altogether; a calm, serene child, attached to reality, begins to work out his elevation through work. Normalisation has been achieved.”

- Maria Montessori, The Secret of Childhood, p.162.

Height of Tables and Chairs

“When we speak of ‘environment’ we include the sum total of objects which a child can freely choose and use as he pleases, that is to say, according to his needs and tendencies. A teacher simply assists him at the beginning to get his bearings among so many different things and teaches him the precise use of each of them, that is to say, she introduces him to the ordered and active life of the environment. But then she leaves him free in the choice and execution of his work.”

- Maria Montessori, The Discovery of the Child, p. 65.

In an environment containing 25 children, with an age span ranging from 3 to 6, it’s necessary to have at least two sizes of table and chair for the simple reason that a 6-year-old is considerably bigger than a 3-year-old. The smaller tables should be about 45cm (18 inches) high, and the bigger ones about 55cm (22 inches) high, and in a perfect world you can have some halfway between those extremes. You don’t want the table to be too high, just high enough for a child to be able to crawl underneath, should the urge strike. I also try and have at least two sizes of chair, but in one class Beijing I had some particularly small 3-year-olds so I got my hands on a pair of the shortest chairs I’ve ever seen (from an IC/Toddler class), so they could sit comfortably. The interesting thing was: all of the children -even the biggest ones- seemed to enjoy sitting in those extra short chairs. Go figure.

Size of Tabletop

A Cylinder Block is 470mm long and the top of the small tables must be able to hold four Cylinder Blocks arranged in a square. The larger tables must be able to hold the biggest piece of material and in most environments that’s the Division Control Charts, which, depending on the manufacturer, are approximately 80cm (32 inches) long. For the Fabrics presentation, it’s been my experience that the full set - ‘8 to 10 pairs of fabrics’- are too big to fit on most tables, so I often present them on a mat; this isn’t ideal, because of the tactile distinction between the feel of a table surface and the feel of a mat, but on any table except the most massive it’s not practical. Fractions are also on the big side, which is why most children choose to use them on a mat. Most, but not all.

Layout of the Furniture

I’ve worked in well-established schools, with beautifully designed and thoughtfully arranged classrooms, and in others that needed a little adjustment. In Shanghai, the school was well-established, but I spent a week observing the way the children used the materials and interacted with them and each other, reflected upon what worked and what didn’t, then went in on a weekend and moved the shelves and the tables so the class would have a better flow.

BeforeAfter

In 2019 I started working at Times Montessori, in Taiwan. Although the school had been open a few years, they didn’t have a primary class, so I was responsible for preparing a new 3-6 environment. The tables and the shelves had been ordered, but when they arrived, they weren’t ideal, so I was able to order new ones.

Some of the materials were bought from Nienhuis, but I provided all of the paper materials and -once they were printed- I laminated, cut, and put them on the shelves. It took me about 8 weeks to prepare this environment:

It took me 4 weeks to get this room in Beijing to a place where the children could start, but probably another six months to feel like it was actually ‘prepared.’

“The only thing the absorbent mind needs is the life of the individual; give him life and an environment and he will absorb all that is in it. But, of course, if you keep a camera in a drawer, you will never get any pictures. It is necessary for this absorbent mind to go out into the environment”

- Maria Montessori, The 1946 London Lectures, p. 65.

Layout of the Shelves

When I set up a new environment, the first thing I do is unroll the 1000-chain mat: at almost 10 metres it is by far the longest piece of material in the room, and it takes a lot of planning to make sure there’s a long empty stretch of floor where the mat can be laid. Sometimes there isn’t enough room in the room to unroll the mat, and other times the furniture hasn’t been arranged properly, two of the reasons why so many children count the long chains in the hall.

The next activity to plan for is the next in size: Walking on the Line. Ideally the line should be in the shape of an ellipse, but often it isn’t, which is a little surprising, as it’s not that difficult to make an ellipse: all you need are one adult, a pair of Montessori-trained 4-year-olds, some string, a marker and two colleagues or a pair of 5-year-olds. The main purpose of the line is for walking, but as it’s a large space and without obstacles, the whole group can sit there, so I also like to use it for circle time. For this reason, the dimensions of the ellipse are determined by how many children are in the group: a class of ten 3-year-olds won’t take nearly as much space as one containing 20 children. I also make sure either 3 or 4 mats can fit inside the ellipse comfortably, so the children who are sitting at a mat don’t get in the way of others when they walk on the line.

In his 1968 lecture, AM Joosten emphasised that Montessori deliberately avoided creating a detailed curriculum in order to keep the approach adaptable to children's real-world needs, and that the method must be a living response to the unique needs of time and place rather than a rigid set of rules and standards:

"Dr. Montessori has never given a fixed and closed list of these exercises. She was always very careful not to do so, and even resisted requests to do so. Had she done so, she would have closed a door which should remain wide open. Standardization would here mean stagnation. It would turn a help to life into a mere school-subject, an execution of a syllabus instead of a living response to an existential need."

- Joosten, A. M. (2013). ‘Exercises of Practical Life: Introduction and List’. The NAMTA Journal, 38(2), 5, 6. (1968)

I think of these lines from Joosten when I look at the lines on the floor in many Montessori classes where guides believe the line should be used for walking only. This makes zero sense to me, but I understand why they think this way: they’ve been indoctrinated to think this way. There is an AMI training centre in Beijing, and in the practice/demonstration class the trainer laid down an irregular ellipse with thin white tape, then put shelves holding cutting, sewing and land and water forms inside it. In the school attached to the training centre, the teachers without exception laid down an irregular ellipse with thin white tape, then put shelves holding cutting, sewing and land and water forms inside it. Molère!

But I digress … Once the 1000-chain mat and the ellipse have been planned for, the next thing to be settled is where to place the shelves for each area of the curriculum, but before doing this I think about sleeping and eating. The younger children have to nap every day, and some schools have a designated room with beds, but many don’t, and in that situation they have to sleep in the class. So where to put the beds affects where I put the shelves and the tables, as does where the children will eat lunch, and how many tables will have to be moved before every meal, which in turn affects the size and weight of the tables I order. There’s a lot to plan for.

Layout of Mathematics Area

All Montessori activities move from simple to complex, but in Mathematics the progression is most clearly defined, so I like to place the Maths shelves in a straight line. This layout allows me to add a continuous rim shelf, running the length of the wall above the shelves, where I can display materials such as the Control Charts used in Group 4. During the training we were told the Decimal Beads should be brought to a mat, where the children can then select the quantities they need for the operations (addition, subtraction, etc) but this system rarely goes as smoothly as during the training: the materials are large and cumbersome, and the children are small, so it makes more sense to leave the Decimal Beads on the shelf, where the children can access them easily. So when I lay out the Maths shelves, I lay out the thousands, hundreds, tens and units on the same shelf, from left to right, clearly separated and readily accessible, ideally on a lower shelf so the shorter children are less likely to have a bunch of thousand cubes fall on their head. I like the solution I saw in a class in Beijing: a trolley.

“The first thing his education demands is the provision of an environment in which he can develop the powers given him by nature. This does not mean just to amuse him and let him do what he likes. [… for] development comes from environmental experience.”

- Maria Montessori, The Absorbent Mind, p. 89.

Layout of Language Area

I also like the Sandpaper Letters to be displayed in a long line on a narrow rim, rather than on a vertical display cabinet or hidden in a box on the shelf. In this orientation they don’t take up as much space, and in my experience the letters get used more often when they’re displayed this way. An indirect benefit is that it reinforces the sequence of the letters. A stationery section is very useful, and the children will maintain it as long as it is clear which supplies go where, so if the boxes/drawers aren’t see-through I attach a photo of the contents to each drawer.

I also prepare a table for the Large Moveable Alphabet: conveniently, the letters are the same size as a roll of duct tape, so I use it to put down lines on a big table. As with the Decimal Beads, I’ve found that eliminating the need to bring cumbersome materials to a mat allows the children to just focus on building words, phrases and sentences, and this is hugely appealing to them. As with many aspects of the Montessori approach, it’s not difficult to gauge if you’re doing the right thing: just observe the children, note how often they use the materials and how they use them, then adapt and update accordingly. More on this topic in the Materials - Language section.

Book Corner

Every class needs a place where children can sit and read, whether that’s reading words or looking at the pictures and creating their own story with their imagination. It can be as ostentatious as the one I came across at a Montessori school in Lucerne or as simple and neat as these schools I visited in Asia.

Easel

There are many writing activities when the Easel comes in handy, so I try to place it between the Language and Art areas, where it attracts children from the smallest to the biggest.

Layout of Sensorial Area - Bells

When deciding where to place the bells, the two key factors to consider are:

  • The size of the table: it’s one of the biggest (and most expensive) things in the room
  • How the bells are used: next to book corner isn’t ideal, and neither is close by the snack table where the children eating will be entertained but very likely be unable to resist chatting with the bell-ringer, or the Peace Table, which needs to be peaceful and quiet and calm.

But I enjoy it when the sound of the bells fills the class, so I also don’t like environments where they’re stuck in some far corner, so I like to place them beside a central wall or a window.

Layout of the Practical Life Area

Many PL exercises require access to water and/or a sink (Washing Hands, Washing A Table, Care of Plants, Pouring Liquids, etc, as does the Snack Table) so it’s important to place these shelves near a sink and a faucet, and if you have twins in your class it’s twice as important. As well as shelves for the materials, you also need a shelf for towels and other supplies such as sponges, tissues, etc and you have to figure out what to do with wet towels, cloths, etc. Nienhuis sell what they describe as a ‘Doll Clothes Airer’ and that’s a pretty accurate name as pretty much the only thing you can fit on there are doll clothes. I prefer the old ways, hanging the clothes and towels out on the line.

Brushes, etc.

It can be a challenge, figuring out where to keep the assorted brushes: I’ve found that the more types you have, the more the children are inclined to use them, so I try and have a variety of shapes and sizes available. In my classes in Taipei and in Beijing there was the perfect space, a section of wall where I couldn’t put shelves or tables, but which was ideal for hanging a rail. I try and follow the advice I gave previously (Metal Insets) and keep everything accessible, but the height of the rail is determined by the length of the child-sized mop and broom, so if some children are shorter than this, there’s not much you can do. Besides: necessity is the mother of invention, so children generally find a way to fix most problems.

Display of Polishing Objects and Trays

These days it’s possible to buy some beautiful objects to be polished, so having them on display is both practical and aesthetically pleasing.

“Just because Montessori wishes the directress to be a dynamic link between the children and the environment, she was always careful to avoid saying anything that would destroy the spontaneity and vivacity of the directress in her work, I give very few lessons on how to give lessons, lest my suggestions -becoming stereotyped and parodied- should turn into obstacles instead of help. The directress is dealing with different personalities; and it therefore becomes more a question of how she should orient herself in what is for her a new world, rather than of any rigid or absolute rules.”

- E.M. Standing, Montessori, Her Life and Work, p.285.

This quotation is closely related to the one from Joosten above. And the following photos are from the AMI training centres and classrooms referenced above.

Photo 1 is the polishing shelves created by the trainer and the other photos are of shelves created by their trainees. Imitation is the sincerest form of flattery. The odd thing about these shelves: they look quite pretty and colourful, but I observed a few of those classes and over the course of three hours never saw a child choose a polishing exercise.

Woodwork

Fortunately, my trainers didn’t have a woodwork corner, and my boss in Geneva wasn’t an AMI graduate, so I was free to prepare my carpentry corner as I saw fit. I came across a child-sized carpenters’ bench in a second-hand shop, and I took that as a sign from the universe that my children would benefit from the occasional bout of cutting or planing or hammering. If you’re not so fortunate, you can also do a lot with a clamp attached to a table that’s about a metre high.

The Peace Table

I’ve seen two kinds of peace corner: the first is an area devoted to calmness and reflection, in which the child relaxes and is led to meditative thought, the second a place where the children can go to resolve their issues by speaking with and listening to their classmates. Whichever you prefer, it’s better if the Peace Table is located in a quiet, mellow corner of the class, away from the entrance/exit, the bathroom or the shelf with the bells, etc.

Layout of the Tables

Unlike in traditional classrooms, there aren’t rows of desks in a Montessori class, but tables placed individually or in pairs throughout the room, so a child has the option to sit by themself or alongside a friend. That’s the theory anyway, but it doesn’t always work out that way in practice. I’ve visited countless schools with tables placed in isolation from each other because their teacher believed that a child couldn’t concentrate with another child sitting beside them or facing them and I’ve also seen the other extreme, classes with tables for six or more children. I believe the variety is the spice of life, so I like to have individual tables as well as others in pairs, beside or facing each other, and if there are windows I always place tables beside them, because who doesn’t feel happier when they have a view?

Portable tables with fold-away legs are also useful for a variety of activities, including eating lunch, any kind of writing, and taking the class outdoors when the sun is shining.

Single-Use Tables

I like to have two mirrors in the class: one of them wall-mounted and hung at a height suitable for children, and another that is portable and brought to a table, where the children can brush their own or their classmate’s hair, examine their teeth, or very carefully apply some lotion to their face.

The third option is to prepare a table that is only used for self-care, many of which are quite beautiful.

However, the trade-off with having a table that’s only used for one activity is that … it’s only used for one activity. Space is at a premium in most classes, so you have to ask yourself is that the best use of it, especially if you’ve already designated single-use tables for the bells, the farm and the Peace Table? Also, if you go down that road, why not have tables exclusively dedicated to Care of Plants, Washing Cloths, Washing Dishes, Washing Hands and listening to music?

And if you’re in Asia, of course, you really need tables just for calligraphy and making tea.

“The teacher's task is not to talk, but to prepare and arrange a series of motives for cultural activity in a special environment made for the child.”

- Maria Montessori, The Absorbent Mind, p. 7.

Sharing/Nature Table

This table is indispensable: a place where children can bring things they value (a book, a drawing they made, a photo of their family/pet/holiday, a leaf they found in the park …) and share it with their classmates. Parents also get into the spirit of the thing by sending postcards when they go on holiday, and the sharing table is also a nice place to display the photos sent to mark a child’s birthday.

Mats - Quantity

One of the questions I like to ask teachers is, ‘How many mats are required for a class containing 25 children?’ I’ve had responses varying from 8 to 30, which probably says more about the training than the teacher, and I have my own answer, which I’ll keep to myself as it’s generally better to provoke debate than end it, but it’s an important topic for teachers to think about.

Mats - Dimensions

Like the shelves, about 1.1 metre long, so they can comfortably hold the Rods. The height is more difficult to define: your average 3-year-old is pretty small, and I’ve observed them get all twisted up trying to reach the top of the mat without actually stepping on it. Having said that, the mat needs to hold the various Decanomial Square extensions, some of which can take up a fair bit of space.

Mats - Colour

Neutral is better, so the colours of various materials are highlighted, especially Sensorial activities such as Colour Tablets, Decanomial Square and Constructive Triangles, but also Group 3 of Mathematics, with its assorted coloured beads. The children are fairly resilient, but having to use a mat like this does seem to test that a little too much …

Mats - Material and Storage

These are inextricably linked: the more solid mats can stand easily, but they’re not easy to carry -for your average 3-year-old at least- and, unless you’re Jeff Bezos, price is probably going to be an issue: a solitary Nienhuis mat will set you back more than €70, and a stand that holds five mats goes for north of €300, so you’re looking at €650 for five mats and a place to store them. Multiply that by however many mats you think you need and then add VAT … well, it’s going to put a hole in your pocket. I prefer to have simple cloth mats because they’re easier to carry, unroll/roll, they’re generally beige, and they’re way less expensive. I also like the way they can be stored in beautiful baskets which reflect the local culture. I have fond memories of the workshop I visited in Taiwan, and the seemingly endless array of very beautiful and very reasonably priced baskets they sold. Child not included. The cloth mats in their beautiful baskets also take up less space, which allows you to have place mats in different parts of the environment so they’re more accessible.

Mats - Cleaning

Beating mats is a universally popular activity, especially with those clean-freak Swiss.

Boxes of Materials: open or closed?

When I ask teachers why they keep the lids on the boxes, the most common justification is ‘dust’ and I think to myself, ‘If only there were 25+ children available, children who not only enjoy dusting but have been shown how to do so …’ For me: boxes must be open. Always. The materials are beautiful so why hide them away: out of sight is out of mind. Although hiding them away was pretty much exactly what Montessori did in 1907, when she didn’t know any better.

"The central and main room in the building is the room for 'intellectual work.' The furniture is light so that the children can move it around as they wish. In this working room, there are two indispensable pieces of furniture. One is a very large cupboard with large doors and the learning materials are kept inside this cupboard."

- Maria Montessori, The Discovery of the Child. p. 46. (1909)

Class Decoration

There’s some evidence that Heavily Decorated Classrooms Disrupt Attention and Learning In Young Children, so it’s important not to let decoration get out of control, but what pictures there are should also be hung at the child’s eye level. I have blu-tak available for the children as they enjoy sticking their drawings/metal inset paper/maths calculations/random writings on the wall. Although it can sometimes get a little messy, particularly at the beginning of the year, they soon get better at displaying it, and it can’t get too out of control because they take everything home each Friday.

Raphael’s Madonna della Seggiola (Madonna of the Chair)

“Above the blackboards are hung attractive pictures, chosen carefully, representing simple scenes in which children would naturally be interested. Among the pictures in our “Children’s Houses” in Rome we have hung a copy of Raphael’s “Madonna della Seggiola,” and this picture we have chosen as the emblem of the Children’s Houses. For indeed, these Children’s Houses” represent not only social progress, but universal human progress, and are closely related to the elevation of the idea of motherhood, to the progress of woman and to the protection of her offspring. In this beautiful conception, Raphael has not only shown us the Madonna as a Divine Mother holding in her arms the babe who is greater than she, but by the side of this symbol of all motherhood, he has placed the figure of St. John, who represents humanity. So in Raphael’s picture we see humanity rendering homage to maternity, -maternity, the sublime fact in the definite triumph of humanity. In addition to this beautiful symbolism, the picture has a value as being one of the greatest works of art of Italy’s greatest artist. And if the day shall come when the “Children’s Houses” shall be established throughout the world, it is our wish that this picture of Raphael’s shall have its place in each of the schools, speaking eloquently of the country in which they originated.”

- Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, Pg. 82. (1912) The Discovery of the Child, Pg. 47 (1947)

Bathroom

It’s useful if the bathroom is inside the class, and better still if the facilities are child-sized, but even if they aren’t, children are quite ingenious.

Storage and Admin

In her article on Preparing the Adult’s Environment, Julia Volkman writes: ‘Two important areas that are often overlooked in the design of a classroom environment are the need for storage (for the guide’s personal items, for the didactic materials that are not currently in use and for consumable supplies that will be reﬁlled during the day) and privacy.’ The materials need to be rotated throughout the year, so it’s important to be able to access them: messy or disorganised storage spaces result in a lot of wasted time and energy. The first two photos are of the storage I reorganised in Shanghai, the others of the store-room in my class in Beijing. When I started there was a jumble of odds and ends in the class, which looked awful and took up valuable space that should have been the children’s, so I measured the empty space to the left of the grey cabinets and then bought, assembled and installed the shelves, the dimensions perfectly fitting the oven that was previously kept in the class. I also bought the boxes and added labels to each.

Trillium Montessori are an excellent resource for teachers, with lots of sound advice and ideas, including how to store different small items such as beans, Arts and Crafts supplies, etc. I like to have a small basket that holds the plastic pouches the children bring home at the end of every week, and for storing children’s unfinished papers, etc a tall cabinet is invaluable, as it’s easy to label (although I prefer my labels to have photos of the children alongside their names) and doesn’t take up a lot of space. You can tell a lot about how organised/disorganised a class is by opening a few drawers.
